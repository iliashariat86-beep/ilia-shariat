How Levees Work and Where They Began

ilia October 31, 2026

In 1953, a devastating flood struck parts of the Netherlands. The disaster claimed 1,836 lives and killed approximately 187,000 animals, including livestock and poultry. It also destroyed around 4,300 buildings and damaged another 43,000.

However, flooding was not a new phenomenon in the Netherlands. Due to its geographical location and the fact that large parts of the country lie below sea level, the Netherlands has faced the threat of flooding from the sea and rivers for centuries.

This disaster marked a turning point in Dutch civil engineering. In response, the government launched major infrastructure projects to improve flood protection and safeguard coastal areas.

Before exploring these large-scale projects, let's take a look at older structures that have been used for centuries to prevent flooding. These structures are known as levees.

What Is a Levee?

A levee is a man-made structure designed primarily to reduce the frequency of flooding in a specific area of a floodplain.

Levees come in different forms. They can be built as earthen embankments, concrete structures, or a combination of both.

They are generally built along waterways and often connect to higher ground at both ends. Some are also constructed as ring-shaped structures to protect specific areas.

How Do Earthen Levees Work?

Earthen levees are usually built using layers of soil that are carefully placed and compacted to create a strong embankment.

The soil is selected based on its properties. Clay-rich soils are often used because they have low permeability, meaning they allow less water to pass through.

The embankment is usually wider at its base and narrower at the top, providing stability against water pressure.

When water levels rise, the levee acts as a physical barrier, keeping floodwater away from protected areas. Its compacted soil structure helps limit water seepage and maintain stability.

Common Risks of Traditional Levees

Traditional levees, particularly earthen embankments, face two main risks:

  • Overtopping: When water rises above the top of a levee and flows over it.

  • Seepage: When water passes through or underneath the levee.

Both problems can weaken the structure and potentially lead to flooding in nearby communities.

The Idea of Movable Storm Surge Barriers

The 1953 disaster exposed weaknesses in the Netherlands' existing flood defences and led to the development of the Delta Works.

However, some locations presented additional engineering challenges. Simply raising existing levees was not always practical, as it could damage historic sites and require the demolition of nearby buildings.

Another important requirement was keeping waterways open for ships, especially along the route to the Port of Rotterdam.

Engineers needed a solution that could provide flood protection without permanently blocking these waterways. This led to the development of movable storm surge barriers.

A movable barrier is an engineered structure that stays open under normal conditions but closes when the risk of flooding or storm surges becomes high, preventing water from entering protected areas.

But how does such a massive structure work?

The Maeslant Storm Surge Barrier

The Maeslant Storm Surge Barrier is still considered a remarkable engineering achievement. Built between 1991 and 1997, it is one of the most impressive movable flood protection structures in the world.

Here are some of its key specifications:

  • Construction: 1991–1997

  • Total width: Approximately 420 metres

  • Height: 22 metres

  • Depth: 15 metres

  • Protection capacity: Designed to withstand storm surges of up to 5 metres above the Dutch reference water level (NAP).

  • Closing operation: The gates sink to the bottom within approximately two hours after being filled with water.

The barrier has two massive gates, each 210 metres wide. Each gate is connected to the shore by a huge spherical joint, measuring 10 metres in diameter and weighing 680 tonnes.

The gates are hollow. When the barrier closes, they are filled with water and gradually sink into position, blocking the waterway.

Once the danger has passed, pumps remove the water, allowing the gates to float back into their open position. This design also allows the gates to be moved into their maintenance docks.

The barrier is designed to close automatically during extreme water conditions. In addition to emergency operation, it undergoes an annual test closure in late September. I highly recommend watching a video of this impressive process!
