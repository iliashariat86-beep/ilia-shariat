Building a House from Start to Finish: A Civil Engineer’s Perspective

ilia October 24, 2026

Hi, I’m Ilia Shariaty, a civil engineering student. Today, we’re going to explore how a civil engineer looks at the process of building a house from start to finish, from initial planning and site preparation to structural work and the final stages of construction. At each stage, we’ll look at what happens and how the project moves forward.

Phase 1: Before Construction Begins

Before building a house, we need to assess the site and its surroundings to determine whether the land is suitable for construction. Four key factors must be considered at this stage.

1. Groundwater Level

Before construction begins, we need to determine how deep the groundwater is and whether it could affect the foundation, soil stability, or the long-term durability of the structure.

Why is this important?

A high groundwater level can make foundation work more challenging and affect the behavior of the soil. Water may also seep into the excavation, making drainage or dewatering necessary. In some cases, groundwater can cause corrosion of construction materials and damage underground structural elements.

2. Soil Investigation

The soil beneath a building plays a crucial role in supporting the structure and transferring its loads to the ground. Before construction begins, the soil must be examined to determine its type and physical and mechanical properties. This includes assessing its bearing capacity, which indicates how much load it can safely support, as well as its compaction and moisture content. Soil settlement must also be considered, as excessive or uneven settlement can lead to structural damage.

3. Bearing Capacity Assessment

Once the soil has been investigated, the next step is to determine whether it can safely support the building's loads. Engineers do this by carrying out field and laboratory tests to understand the soil's properties, then calculating its bearing capacity based on the soil type, ground conditions, and foundation dimensions. They also compare the pressure exerted by the building with the soil's allowable bearing capacity and assess the risk of settlement. These findings help engineers choose a suitable foundation type and determine its dimensions.

4. Environmental Assessment and Site Hazards

Before construction begins, the site's environmental conditions and surrounding area must be assessed to identify potential hazards and understand how the project may affect the environment.

This assessment includes examining local climate conditions, such as rainfall, temperature, humidity, wind, and freezing temperatures. Natural hazards, including earthquakes, floods, landslides, and soil erosion, must also be considered. In addition, the surrounding vegetation, wildlife, and water resources should be evaluated, along with any potential contamination of the soil, groundwater, or air.

Understanding these factors helps engineers identify site limitations, reduce risks, and plan construction more safely and effectively.

 

Phase 2: Foundation Construction

After determining the exact building location and completing the initial site investigations, the ground must be prepared for foundation construction. The foundation transfers the building's loads to the soil, and its proper construction is essential for structural stability.

Main Stages of Foundation Construction

1. Topsoil Removal

At this stage, the top layer of soil, which typically contains plant roots, organic matter, and loose soil, is removed from the construction site. These materials can reduce the stability of the foundation base and contribute to future soil settlement.

This operation is usually carried out using machinery such as excavators and bulldozers. The depth of topsoil removal depends on the site conditions and the findings of the geotechnical investigation.

2. Excavation

At this stage, the soil is excavated to the depth and dimensions specified in the construction drawings. This creates the space needed to build the foundation according to the engineering design and ensures that the structure can be constructed on a suitable base.

3. Footing Construction

A footing is the base of a foundation, typically located beneath columns or load-bearing walls. Its purpose is to transfer the building's loads to the soil and distribute them over a larger area, reducing the pressure on the ground.

For example, snowshoes spread a person's weight over a larger surface area, preventing them from sinking too deeply into the snow. In a similar way, footings distribute building loads over a wider area of soil, helping to limit excessive settlement.

4. Foundation Walls

After the footings have been constructed, the foundation walls are built to transfer loads from the upper parts of the building to the footings. These walls are commonly made of reinforced concrete to provide sufficient strength against structural loads and lateral earth pressure.

In buildings with basements, foundation walls also retain the surrounding soil and prevent it from collapsing into the basement. In addition, when these walls are in contact with moist soil, proper waterproofing is essential to prevent water from entering the building.

Additional Foundation Features

Depending on the site conditions, local climate, and building design, the following additional measures may be necessary to protect the foundation

1. Thermal Insulation

Foundation thermal insulation uses materials that reduce heat transfer between the building and the surrounding soil. This helps maintain indoor temperatures, reduce energy consumption for heating and cooling, and protect below-ground areas from temperature changes. The need for thermal insulation depends on the local climate and the building design.

2. Waterproofing

Waterproofing involves applying protective coatings and using waterproof materials to prevent moisture and groundwater from penetrating the foundation and below-ground parts of the building. This helps reduce the risk of reinforcement corrosion, concrete deterioration, and moisture problems in basements. The type of waterproofing and installation method depend on soil conditions, groundwater levels, and the building design.

3. Drainage System

A drainage system collects and redirects surface water and groundwater to prevent water from accumulating around the foundation. By reducing water pressure against basement walls and limiting moisture infiltration, it helps protect the structure and reduce the risk of water-related damage. The drainage system's design depends on soil conditions, groundwater levels, and site characteristics.

Phase Three: Structural Framing and Building Services

At this stage, the building’s main structural elements, including columns, beams, and floor slabs, are constructed. The walls and main components of the building’s external envelope are then put in place to protect the interior from weather conditions.

As construction progresses, building services are also installed according to the technical drawings. These include water supply and drainage pipes, electrical wiring, and heating, cooling, and ventilation systems (HVAC).
